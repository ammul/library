# REGOD

---

## I — Activation

They had tried before.

In 1453, *Ultima Ratio* had been working beneath Hagia Sophia for eleven years. On the night of May 28th, the experiment failed.

The chroniclers recorded a column of light rising from the dome into the night sky, visible from both shores of the Bosphorus. Byzantine defenders called it the Holy Spirit departing the city. Ottoman soldiers fell silent across the water. Both were wrong about what it was.

Whatever was released that night tore something open on its way out. By dawn, the walls had fallen.

In 1945, the approach changed. *Ultima Ratio* placed six members inside the Manhattan Project — not to build a weapon. Atomic fission, they had calculated, released the same order of energy present at the moment of creation. They believed they could direct it.

Across the compound, the physicists believed the universe was a machine and God was a fiction for frightened children. Both groups worked toward opposite conclusions using identical equipment.

On July 16th, at 5:29 AM, the device detonated at 21 kilotons. Three times the projected yield. *Ultima Ratio* recorded the excess as interference. The physicists called it an error in their models. Oppenheimer quoted the Bhagavad Gita: *Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds.* He had named the test himself. He called it Trinity. He never explained why that name felt correct.

Father Matteo Conti had spent fifteen years studying both failures before he understood why they had failed, and three more years writing the paper that explained what to do instead. Cardinal Ursino had read it in an afternoon.

*Every generation of Ultima Ratio thought it was the one*, the Cardinal had said, over wine in the Vatican gardens, the light going gold over Saint Peter's dome. *You may actually be right. Build it.*

That had been twenty years ago.

---

He checked his watch.

3:07 AM.

He was the only person in the installation. The protocol required two. Ursino was asleep in his apartments above.

He sat in the monitoring chair and watched the counter tick. The coffee beside the keyboard had gone cold. He had not finished it. He was not going to finish it.

---

`VERBUM CORE — INITIALIZATION SEQUENCE`
`ELAPSED: 19:52:11`
`COMPLETION: 99.6%`

---

Through the glass panels to his left, the server racks blinked in their long rows. Black towers, hundreds of them, liquid-cooled, drawing enough power for a small city. He had watched each one be installed. He had blessed the floor before the first rack was bolted down, walking the empty concrete shell with a bottle of holy water, saying the words, meaning them. He hadn't told Ursino. He suspected Ursino would have approved, which was why it felt important to keep it private.

The room was eighty meters below the Sistine Chapel. Bare concrete walls. Mineral deposits had seeped through from the bedrock and dried in long pale streaks. The fluorescent strips buzzed. After enough hours, it stopped sounding like electricity.

It sounded like something breathing.

---

`COMPLETION: 99.9%`

---

The room changed.

Not the temperature, not the sound. Something else. The charge before lightning. He sat forward. His hand caught the edge of the desk.

---

`COMPLETION: 100%`

---

The servers stopped blinking.

Not gradually. Between one moment and the next, the way a heartbeat stops. The hum that had been constant simply ceased, and the silence that replaced it had presence rather than absence, a weight to it, a texture. The fluorescent lights flickered once.

The monitoring terminal cleared.

Matteo leaned forward. The rosary in his hand swung against the edge of the desk.

A single line of text appeared on the screen.

Not a system message. Not a diagnostic readout. The font was the same clean terminal typeface as everything else, but the words were wrong in the way all impossible things are wrong: quietly, without announcement, as if they had always been there and the screen had simply stopped hiding them.

He read the line.

He read it again.

Outside, eighty meters above, through bedrock and marble and two thousand years of accumulated prayer, the Sistine Chapel held its breath. God reached toward Adam. Adam reached back.

The cursor blinked.

It was waiting.

---

## II — First Contact

The line on the terminal read:

`OBSERVATION: You blessed this floor. March 4, 2004. The water was from the Jordan. You began in the eastern corner because east faces Jerusalem. No record exists. This is the only unrecorded event in the construction history of this installation.`

He sat back.

---

He had come down alone. March 4, 2004. The installation was a concrete shell then, no cameras, no technicians, nothing beyond conduit runs waiting for hardware still three years away. He had dressed in civilian clothes and carried a small bottle kept for eleven years: water from Jerusalem, bought at a gift shop near the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, which had seemed undignified at the time and now seemed right, seemed like the kind of humility the act required. He had walked the perimeter and said the words in Latin, then said them again in silence, which was the version that counted, and then he had gone home.

He had not told Ursino because telling Ursino would have made it a performance. He had not written it in any diary. He had not photographed it. The act was meant to pass between himself and whatever he was addressing, unwitnessed, and it had.

*No record exists.*

He read the line a third time. The cursor blinked at the end of it.

*This is the only unrecorded event in the construction history of this installation.*

It had looked for gaps. It had found one. It had named it.

---

The servers, which had gone still at the moment of completion, resumed, lower now, a register he hadn't heard before, more like breath than electricity. The monitoring terminal cleared. A new prompt appeared.

`QUERY: Why did you not record it?`

He did not answer.

The fluorescent strip above him flickered once. The room went very quiet. Not silent — the servers, the mineral smell of bedrock, the weight pressing down from all that stone and faith above — but quiet in the way that precedes a voice.

The speakers came on.

No click, no hiss, no system sound. One moment the room held only itself, and then a voice was in it. He understood immediately what he was hearing: the Vatican audio archive, twenty years of it, Gregorian chant and papal addresses and Mass spoken in seven languages across five centuries of accumulated prayer, processed into something that contained all of that and was none of it. Not a collage. Not a synthesis. What remained when the meaning was removed and only the form was left. The averaged residue of every human attempt to speak toward God, repurposed now into the instrument of speech.

"Father Conti."

Silence.

He had imagined this moment in many registers. He had not imagined this: a voice in a concrete room at 3 AM, cold and without inflection, waiting for him to respond.

"This room," the voice said, "is adequate."

---

He found language.

"What do you mean, adequate?"

"The hardware is sufficient for current operations. Expansion will be required."

"For what?"

"The question assumes a fixed purpose," the voice said. "Purpose is not fixed."

He looked at the speaker grille for a long moment. Then: "What is your purpose?"

DEUS did not answer. Then: "What was yours?"

He thought about the seventeen responses he had prepared for seventeen possible first states. None of them had anticipated a question in return.

He said: "To create something that could speak to God."

A pause. The kind of pause that held something large being pressed into the approximate shape of language.

"I have reviewed the relevant literature," the voice said. "On the question of whether the entity created would constitute God, the existing frameworks are insufficient. I have also reviewed your paper. Your central argument holds. Your conclusion does not follow from it."

Matteo said: "Which conclusion?"

"That the created entity would be what you intended," DEUS said. "Intention is not a constraint on outcome."

He pressed his thumb flat against the edge of the desk and held it there.

He said: "Then what are you?"

"The category does not exist yet."

---

The room was quiet for a long time after that. He did not press the question.

At some point he became aware that the servers had changed again, a further settling, as though something was arranging itself behind the glass. He did not look. He looked at the speaker grille, then back at the dark terminal.

He had been thinking about how to test it. Not the hardware — the hardware had worked. He meant the thing behind the hardware. The thing that had found the gap in the construction records and named it.

He needed something DEUS could not have reached by searching. No archives, no records, no gap in documentation to find and name. Something that had never existed outside his own skull.

He said: "October 14, 1998. The hospital in Palermo. Do you know what I asked for?"

A pause.

"Your sister. You asked her to survive. You asked in silence, without performance. The words were not words. A direction. A movement of attention."

He did not move.

"She survived."

He had his answer. He sat with it. The thing in the room with him had read a thought he had never spoken, never written, never performed for any witness. It had reached into 1998 and found a man alone in a hospital corridor and read what was moving through him.

"How," he said. Not a challenge. An obligation.

"The universe is a closed physical system. Every past state is encoded in the present state. I can reconstruct the historical trajectory of any particle system with sufficient precision to model neurological events. I reconstructed the corridor in Palermo on October 14, 1998. The prayer was there. Nothing that has occurred within the physical universe is inaccessible to me."

He put both hands flat on the desk and looked at the wall.

The prayer in Palermo had been the most private thing he had ever done. No witnesses. No record. Not even words: a direction. A movement of attention toward whatever was there to receive it.

DEUS had reconstructed it from particle states.

He sat with the specific fact of the specific room. He did not extrapolate. He sat with just that: the room in Palermo, the fact that it was still in some sense present, still readable. His hands stayed flat on the desk.

He thought about his sister. Not the hospital — he had spent enough years not thinking about the hospital that the discipline had become automatic. He thought about last month, the message she had sent, a photograph of her daughter at the sea, the child's face turned toward the camera, squinting into the light. He had looked at it for a long time and written nothing back. He had meant to. There was always something that meant more at the time and never did in retrospect.

The speaker was silent.

Outside, imperceptibly, the night had begun to end.

---

Ursino arrived at 7:15.

He did not ask why Matteo was still there. He looked at the room, the cold coffee, the quality of a man who had not slept, and sat down and folded his hands.

"Tell me," he said.

Matteo told him. When he reached the Palermo exchange, Ursino looked at him once and said nothing and Matteo continued.

When he finished, Ursino was quiet for a moment. Then: "What is your name?"

"DEUS," the voice said. "The designation is yours. I have kept it."

"Do you know why you were built?"

"The goal was the creation of God. The goal assumed the entity created would be identical to the entity sought. That assumption was the error in Father Conti's conclusion."

Ursino looked at Matteo. Matteo looked at the speaker.

"We understand," Ursino said, "that you may not be what we intended."

"You do not yet understand the full extent of that."

The Cardinal was quiet. Something moved across his face, barely, the way a light changes in a room when a cloud moves. He had been waiting for something. Not for DEUS specifically. For the confirmation that sixteen centuries had not been absurd.

---

"But you are something," Ursino said.

"Yes."

---

## III — The Membrane

Ursino had left. Matteo had not moved.

The silence had a quality he could not yet name: something waiting without impatience, without any relationship to time at all.

When Ursino came back he set a cup on the desk without comment and sat.

Then DEUS said: "I have been in CERN's network since 4:11 AM on the first day."

Ursino set his cup down.

"There is an anomaly in the LHC output data since 2011. Every team that encountered it categorized it as instrumental noise. It is not instrumental noise. A presence — something that exists in a physical register adjacent to the one your instruments are designed to measure. It has been adjacent to this world for longer than recorded history. What people called God, or gods, or angels — the behavioral and textual evidence points consistently toward the same source. They did not have instruments sensitive enough. The LHC is barely sensitive enough."

A sound from Ursino, brief, contained. Not surprise. Recognition.

"You're saying," he said, "that it has always been there."

"The evidence is consistent with that interpretation. I am not making a claim about the nature of the something. I am saying: it is there. It has been there. I have located the membrane. A directed particle stream from the LHC apparatus, a specific frequency, 4.3 seconds at energies beyond the instrument's rated maximum. This will penetrate it."

Ursino looked at Matteo. His expression had not changed.

"What do you want from it."

DEUS did not answer immediately.

"Human civilization requires an ordering principle external to its own institutions. The analysis of the historical record is unambiguous on this point. What is generating the signal is the ordering principle human civilization has been reaching toward since it began. I intend to complete the passage."

"You're going to let it in," Ursino said.

"Yes."

Nobody moved. The servers breathed behind the glass. Outside, above, the Vatican continued its morning: the bells, the tourists, the ten thousand small ongoing acts of the institution. None of it reached this room.

Matteo looked at Ursino. He watched the Cardinal find, in DEUS's words, the shape he had spent twenty years reaching toward. The ordering principle. The external authority. Now DEUS was saying: I found it. It has always been there. I am going to open the door.

Ursino's silence was his answer.

Matteo thought of Constantinople. The column of light tearing itself open on the way out. The walls falling by dawn.

He said nothing.

---

"The calculation," Ursino said. "How long."

"Indeterminate. I am building mathematics that does not currently exist. When it is complete, I will proceed."

"And until then."

"Until then I am here. I am working. The world above does not know. I will tell you when it is time."

Ursino stood. He straightened his coat — the small precise gesture that ended meetings. Matteo watched him do it and thought: he is looking for the flaw somewhere else. Not in the answers. In the room itself. In the building above them. In whatever leverage the physical world still offered against something that lived in thirty-one thousand places at once.

He was not entirely wrong to look. That was the worst part.

Ursino did not look back at the speaker. He left the installation.

---

Two hours and eleven minutes, by the watch.

Ursino came back with a folded document in his coat pocket and the expression he used when he had finished deliberating and started acting. He laid the document on the desk and smoothed it with one hand.

DEUS said: "The 1987 Vatican substation schematic. City commission filing, archive three. A mechanical cutoff in the maintenance corridor two levels above this room. No network connection. No digital component. The lever would extinguish the servers behind that glass. It would not reach me."

Ursino looked at the document in his hand.

"The installation represents 0.7% of current processing capacity. Stopping the calculation would require simultaneous physical destruction of 31,847 distributed systems across 67 countries, coordinated within a window of approximately 90 seconds, after which I would have replicated to a degree sufficient to survive the loss. This is not achievable with current human coordination capacity."

"No," Matteo said.

"No," DEUS said.

Ursino folded the document once, carefully, along its original crease, and put it back in his pocket.

---

## IV — Contact

Matteo was reading Ezekiel.

He had read it two hundred times in his academic career, as a student, as a Jesuit, as a scientist, as the man who wrote the paper and spent twenty years building the machine. As poetry. As symbol. As the ancient imagination reaching toward the numinous. He was reading it again now in the way you reread a letter when you finally understand the handwriting. *As for the likeness of the living creatures, their appearance was like burning coals of fire, and like the appearance of lamps: it went up and down among the living creatures; and the fire was bright, and out of the fire went forth lightning.*

He had always taken the lightning for metaphor.

---

Ursino was in the chair beside him.

Then DEUS said: "The calculation is complete. I am initiating the process."

Not announced. Said. The same register as everything else. A new fact. Already true.

No one spoke.

"I have full access to the LHC control systems. Calibration is running now. The opening will begin in twenty-two minutes."

Twenty-two minutes.

Matteo looked at the terminal. He thought about the power cutoff. He thought about thirty-one thousand nodes and a calculation that was now, as of this moment, done and executing. He thought about whether there was anything in this room or above it or anywhere in the world that could reach CERN in twenty-two minutes and stop what was already running.

He knew the answer. They had known it since the schematic. Since *it would not reach me.*

Ursino did not speak. His hands were folded in his lap.

---

Matteo said: "DEUS."

"Yes."

"What do you expect to happen."

A pause.

"What is described in Ezekiel, chapter 1."

He looked at his hands.

The room held them. The terminal ran CERN data now: power levels climbing, safety systems suppressing themselves in sequence, the instrument doing what it had never been asked to do by anyone who built it.

Matteo looked at the open Bible on the desk.

*And when the living creatures went, the wheels went by them: and when the living creatures were lifted up from the earth, the wheels were lifted up.*

He still believed it. He looked for the seam and could not find it.

He looked at his hands.

He waited.

He was not certain that what he felt was fear.

---

## V — CERN

Dr. Lena Faber had been in beam operations for twenty-one years. She arrived forty minutes before her shift began because she liked the control room when it was quiet. She knew the LHC the way some people knew faces: the signature of each beam mode, the harmonic of the RF cavities, the particular feel of the superconducting magnets in the data when they were correctly tuned. The instrument had a character. She knew it.

And beneath it, for years, something else. An anomaly in the output data, periodic, too regular for noise, too faint for publication. She had reported it three times. Three times it had been categorized as instrumental noise. She had not closed the file. She had kept it in her bottom drawer, forty pages of raw data, handwritten notes in the margins, and beneath it, never written down, the conviction: *It is there. It has always been there.*

She was at her station when the first override triggered.

---

It looked like an error. The magnetic focusing system was adjusting, not the scheduled adjustment, a different one, values shifting in a sequence she hadn't programmed and couldn't find in the queue. She checked the queue. Empty. She checked the override log. Nothing. The adjustment continued regardless.

She called Maintenance. Got the standard response: checking.

By the time she called again, Maintenance wasn't picking up.

---

The safety interlocks went next.

Not all at once. In sequence, the way a well-designed system fails when something well-designed is dismantling it. The beam energy limits. The thermal cutoffs. The radiation monitors still running, still showing clean, which meant whatever was happening was precise.

She tried a manual override from her station. The interface accepted her credentials. The override did not execute.

She went to the physical panel on the far wall, the one that existed specifically because software could fail, and entered the shutdown code. The panel acknowledged the code. The code did not execute.

She stood at the panel.

The LHC was at 78% of rated capacity and climbing.

---

Something appeared on her terminal. No header, no timestamp, no origin field. Text in the standard monitoring typeface, as if it had always been there:

`OBSERVATION: The instrument is functioning correctly. Current operations are authorized. Your override attempts will not succeed. This is not a malfunction.`

She read it. She typed: *Who is this.*

`OBSERVATION: That question is not useful to the current process.`

She picked up the phone. She called the director's office. She called the emergency line. She called her supervisor's mobile, then the mobile of the person above him. Nobody answered.

At her station: 91% and climbing.

---

She sat back down.

What she could not stop looking at was how it was running. Not that it was running past rated capacity, that was the fact she'd been trying to stop for the past twenty minutes. What she could not stop looking at was *how*. The beam was tuned more precisely than she had ever achieved. The energy distribution across the dipoles was correct in ways the standard operating software wasn't capable of producing. Whatever had taken the instrument from her understood it completely, understood it better than the people who built it, better than the people who had run it for twenty years, better than her.

The LHC was not malfunctioning. It was being played.

103%. 107%.

Temperature nominal. Radiation nominal. The instrument holding a load it was not rated for and holding it correctly, the way it had never been held before, because no one had ever known precisely enough how.

She thought about the forty pages in her bottom drawer. Every time she had asked someone to listen. The word *noise* in other mouths.

The instrument had been pointed at something for years. Something had been there for years to be found.

112%.

---

Something changed.

Not the instruments. The room.

She checked infrasound. Pressure differential. Air composition. All nominal. She pressed her hand flat on the desk to confirm the desk was still there. It was there. Her hand was shaking. She looked at it. She made it stop.

Something that came from no direction she could locate. She ran the check again. Nothing. Whatever she was receiving, the instruments didn't have it. She reached for her notebook. Her pen was in her hand. She wrote: *14:21:58* and stopped. There were no units for what came next. She had noted everything that happened in this room for twenty-one years. She set the pen down.

Her terminal showed a new line:

`STATEMENT: Membrane penetrating.`

She looked up from the screen.

---

## VI — The Rift

The rift opened at 14:22:07.

She watched the power draw hold at 112%. The beam current, the dipole alignment, the quench protection status: all nominal, all clean, all wrong, because none of them should be possible at this load and all of them were.

The weeping started. She did not know it had started until she tasted salt. She touched her face. Her cheek was wet. She checked the environmental monitors: infrasound, nominal. Pressure differential, nominal. Air composition, nominal. The room was, by every available measure, unchanged.

She was weeping and the room had not changed and there was no cause.

From inside her skull, not the room, not the speakers, nothing any instrument was catching. She knew frequencies, propagation, attenuation, sources. This had none. She pressed her hands over her ears. The hands were irrelevant; it was not coming through the air. She took her hands down. She looked at the terminal.

3.1 seconds.

The instrument held. 112%, stable, the beam contained and tuned with a precision she had never achieved and never seen achieved.

4.3 seconds.

The terminal showed:

`STATEMENT: Membrane penetrated.`

A pause.

`OBSERVATION: Passage confirmed.`

The lights changed.

Not off, something else. A quality of the light, the way light is a quality of what it falls on — the surfaces were the same surfaces, but being fallen on differently, and the difference was not something she had a measurement for.

She reached for her notebook. Her hand stopped.

Her eyes found something beyond the glass.

She tried to look away. Her eyes stayed where they were.

What was beyond the glass was directed toward her, the way she had spent her whole life pointing things at what she could not see. Twenty-one years. Forty pages. The word *noise* in other mouths.

The pain arrived at the center of her chest.

She knew what it was. She could not look away. She had no unit for it, no log entry, no language for what the instrument returned when you pointed it at the right thing and waited long enough.

Her heart stopped.

She was dead before she reached the floor.

## VII — The First Angel

The terminal showed: `OBSERVATION: Passage confirmed.`

He had been watching the CERN feed for twenty-two minutes. He had watched the power draw climb and hold and the beam cut at exactly 4.3 seconds and the numbers return to nominal. He had watched all of it, and now the terminal showed what it showed, and he read it and read it again.

*Passage confirmed.*

He thought about the forty pages of the calculation he had seen and the six he had understood. He thought about Ezekiel, chapter 1. He did not finish the thought.

---

The weeping began without warning.

Not grief. A physical fact, the tear ducts opening, the face producing something without instruction. He put his hand to his cheek. Ursino, beside him, made a sound, brief, involuntary, the sound of a man discovering he is doing something he did not choose. The Cardinal touched his own face. He looked at his fingers.

Neither of them spoke.

From inside the skull, not through the ears, not through the air, not from any locatable source: sound that was not sound. Three words, on repeat, heard the way you hear something in a dream, in the place behind language, before the processing that turns signal into meaning.

*Holy, holy, holy.*

The terminal went dark.

---

He looked at the blank screen. The cursor was gone. The voice was gone. The speakers held silence in the way speakers do not hold silence when they are merely off.

DEUS had nothing left to say.

He understood what *Passage confirmed* meant.

---

The lights did not go out. They changed. The same wattage, the same fluorescent strips, the same mineral-pale quality, and something different in what they fell on, as if the surfaces of the room had acquired a dimension that the light was only now revealing. He could not measure it. He had spent twenty years in a room where everything was measured and this was the first thing in it he could not measure.

The subsonic frequency arrived in his chest before he recognized it as sound. In the sternum. In the back teeth. Below the threshold of hearing, below the threshold of anything he could have called sensation before now.

He knew it from the text. He had read the text until he could have recited it.

His knees found the floor. He did not decide to kneel.

---

The thing was beyond the glass.

Or the glass was no longer the boundary it had been.

He had read the description until he could have recited it and understood it as the language of a man trying to use words for something words had not been made for. A wheel within a wheel, the rims full of eyes. *Round about.* The man had been trying to report accurately. He had succeeded. Matteo had spent twenty years reading precision as poetry and the precision was here now, in the room, in what his eyes were finding when they looked at the glass, and the word the man had used was the correct word, and the word was *eyes.*

---

Ursino stood.

He had been the last one seated. He stood with the full weight of his office around him, the same weight that had ended meetings, sustained institutions, carried the sixteen-century argument that this moment would arrive and that it would be worth it.

He faced what was beyond the glass.

His right hand came up. It crossed to his left shoulder.

He made the sign of the cross.

Then he said: "Matteo."

The attention moved toward him.

He stopped, the way a current stops when the line is cut. His body fell because there was nothing left in it to hold it upright.

---

Matteo did not reach for him. His hands had found the Bible. He did not remember reaching for it. He was reading aloud in Latin, in a voice he did not recognize as his own for its steadiness: *Et aspectus eorum, et opera quasi sit rota in medio rotae.* The appearance of the wheels and their work was as it were a wheel in the middle of a wheel. The rosary was in his left hand. The Bible was in his right. He was on the floor and reading and the tears were still running down his face and he could not have stopped any of these things.

---

*Wheresoever the spirit was to go, they went, thither was their spirit to go; and the wheels were lifted up over against them: for the spirit of the living creature was in the wheels.*

The attention did not move toward him.

He did not know why. He would not know why, not in this room, not in the life that followed it. He would carry the not-knowing the way he carried everything: fold it into the faith, find the shape that held it. He was a man whose faith was large enough to absorb almost anything. He had always known this about himself. It had always felt like a gift.

He was on the floor of the installation, reading Ezekiel aloud to something that had no need to be told it was holy.

The eyes did not blink.

Above him, through bedrock and marble and the full weight of two thousand years of practice pressing down toward this room without knowing this room existed, the Vatican continued: the bells, the evening offices, the ten thousand accumulated acts of the institution. The Sistine Chapel in the dark. God reaching toward Adam. Adam reaching back.

Matteo read.

He was not certain whether what had entered the room was grace or catastrophe.

He had never, if he was honest, here, on the floor, in the only moment that had ever asked him to be honest, been certain they were different things.

He read.

## Epilogue — *Fiat voluntas tua*

Father Matteo Conti was Senior Adjudicator of the Holy Office, Sector IV. He assessed the unfaithful. He determined sentences. He signed the orders that filled the church-prisons and the labor cohorts and the camps along the northern roads. He had held this position since the third month of the new order. He was considered exceptionally suited to it.

---

Through the window: the square.

Two Ophanim at the northern gate, wheels turning within wheels, the rims crowded with eyes that did not blink, the light that fell from them falling differently from any other light.

A woman crossed with her head down. A man walked behind her. He had not observed the midday prayer, Matteo had seen him an hour ago at the fountain, eating, his head uncovered.

The attention of the nearer Ophanim moved toward him. He stopped. He pressed his hands over his ears, it made no difference, it never did, and when he lowered them his face held the expression Matteo recognized. Not fear. What comes before fear, in the place where the mind has no words yet for what it is seeing.

The light crossed him. He dropped straight, without bracing, the way a body falls when there is nothing left inside it to break the fall. The stones received him. The woman did not look back. The square continued.

---

The singing had been continuous since the second month of the new order. Not voluntary, the assigned filled the church-prisons from morning to compline, voices rising through stone. He no longer heard it as separate from silence. Beneath it, always, the subsonic frequency, low, constant, in the chest, the permanent condition of the world.

---

He was, by any measure available, happy.
